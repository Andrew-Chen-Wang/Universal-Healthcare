Compare approaches

The same problems, answered five ways: from changing nothing, through small fixes and bigger reforms that keep today's insurers, to one public payer. Each column is built by a rule from how much each path changes today's system, so no column is tuned by hand.

How the columns are built
Every solution path is labeled no change, minor, moderate, or major (hover a label for what each means). For each of today's 55 problems, a column takes every path at the largest scale it allows, skipping any that conflict with what it already has. Problems a reform creates get the smallest change that answers them. The last column is the site's draft picks, which Claude made for the owner to review. Open any column in the plan builder to change it.The site's spending row prices the single payer with the full cost ledger, and the other columns line by line on top of today's spending (coverage, provider prices, drug prices, new benefits, insurer overhead, workforce), with the same rates and prices. Both are 2024 dollars as if fully in place in 2024. Picks with no cost input, such as antitrust or privacy rules, aren't priced. For a check from outside, the published-estimate row uses one study that priced current law and eight reforms the same way: the Urban Institute's 2019 comparison, for 2020 in 2020 dollars.

What it is

Change nothing. Every problem continues on its current course, and today's insurers, programs, and taxes stay as they are.

Fix what can be fixed with rules, standards, and funding inside today's programs and insurers. Most people keep the coverage they have now.

Add new public programs and payment rules alongside today's insurers: a public option, automatic enrollment, caps on commercial prices, and drug negotiation.

Everyone must have a plan, and competing non-profit or tightly regulated insurers must take everyone at standard benefits, as in Germany, Switzerland, and the Netherlands. Other problems get the same moderate changes as the previous column.

One public payer for everyone, with no cost sharing, financed by payroll and income taxes. This was the site's first draft; it stays as a complete path to compare.

Employer plans stay, and employers with 50+ workers must keep offering them. Everyone else is enrolled automatically in non-profit plans with income-based premiums. Basic dental is in every plan; long-term care is left for later. New federal costs are paid only by taxes on income over $1,000,000. Claude made these picks to the owner's requirements; none is final.

$5.28T a year, +$5.5B against today

Federal +$16.6B

Line by line
  • Care for the newly covered (+$5.9B): Enrollment is voluntary, so we use the share of the uninsured that Urban's Reform 2 (bigger subsidies and a mandate) covered: 12.1%: 2.5 of 26.7 million uninsured people. About 6.3 million uninsured undocumented immigrants stay ineligible (50.0% of 12.5 million). Someone uninsured all year used $2,443 of care in 2013, against $4,876 for someone insured; grown with spending per person to 2024, that is $4,085. Public-plan coverage raises it by 53.4% (Hadley & Holahan), $2,180 a person, so $5.4B more care, plus 9.6% for the plans' overhead (private and Medicaid managed care today). Federal cost: the whole premium for everyone newly covered, $17.0B, an upper bound, since income-based premiums pay part of it.
  • Medicare Advantage payment fixes (−$12.4B): CBO estimates that using two years of diagnosis data and dropping diagnoses from health risk assessments would save $124.0B over ten years, $12.4B a year on average. Excluding chart-review diagnoses and expanding audits would save more; for scale, MedPAC puts coding intensity at $22.0B in 2026. Plans pass part of the cut through as fewer extra benefits, so national spending falls by about the same amount.
  • Workforce capacity investment (+$12.0B): Pays for 14,000 residency slots at $150,000 each, 10,000 nursing faculty, 60,000 clinical placements, 32,000 tuition-free service scholars, and 60,000 community health workers. Without it, the utilization increase turns into longer waits.

$5.29T a year, +$8.9B against today

Federal +$505.5B

Line by line
  • Care for the newly covered (+$63.8B): Everyone eligible is enrolled automatically: 26.7 of 26.7 million uninsured people. Undocumented immigrants are eligible. Someone uninsured all year used $2,443 of care in 2013, against $4,876 for someone insured; grown with spending per person to 2024, that is $4,085. Public-plan coverage raises it by 53.4% (Hadley & Holahan), $2,180 a person, so $58.2B more care, plus 9.6% for the plans' overhead (private and Medicaid managed care today). Federal cost: the whole premium for everyone newly covered, $183.3B, an upper bound, since income-based premiums pay part of it.
  • Cap on commercial prices (−$50.8B): Commercial prices capped at 200.0% of Medicare. Only prices above the cap fall (hospital care: 220.0% of Medicare on $558.9B, −$50.8B). Using the average price understates the savings, since hospitals above the cap lose more than the average suggests.
  • Negotiated drug prices for every payer (−$115.8B): Every payer pays the single-payer model's negotiated price, 72.0% of today's Medicare net price (brand drugs 80.0% of spending, cut 35.0%). Medicaid already pays less through its rebates and keeps its price. No change in use, since copays stay.
  • Dental, vision, and hearing (+$62.3B): A public benefit for dental services, durable medical equipment, at the single-payer model's rates and use: price effect −$14.2B, more use +$70.9B. Private insurance and most out-of-pocket spending for them moves to the public benefit, plus 2.5% administration on $233.2B of new public spending.
  • Long-term care insurance (+$79.3B): A public benefit for nursing facilities & continuing-care communities, other health, residential & personal care, home health care, at the single-payer model's rates and use: price effect +$9.6B, more use +$64.5B. Private insurance and most out-of-pocket spending for them moves to the public benefit, plus 2.5% administration on $210.7B of new public spending.
  • Income-based cap on cost sharing ($0.0B): Spending above each family's cap moves from households to plans: about $127 a person a year, scaled from a study of income-tiered caps on group plans to 2024 spending, $28.6B across 224.8 million people with private insurance. National spending doesn't change except through extra care people use once they reach the cap, which isn't counted. A tighter cap, such as Germany's 2.0% of income, would move more. Its federal cost is counted in the federal side of premium changes.
  • Private insurer overhead (−$29.5B): Overhead stays 12.0% of private benefits, which change by −$245.2B.
  • Medicare Advantage payment fixes (−$12.4B): CBO estimates that using two years of diagnosis data and dropping diagnoses from health risk assessments would save $124.0B over ten years, $12.4B a year on average. Excluding chart-review diagnoses and expanding audits would save more; for scale, MedPAC puts coding intensity at $22.0B in 2026. Plans pass part of the cut through as fewer extra benefits, so national spending falls by about the same amount.
  • Federal side of premium changes ($0.0B): Private premiums change by −$274.7B for people insured today (+$32.0B of it from new benefits and the copay cap, with their overhead). Two federal costs follow premiums. People with premium tax credits (8.6% of the privately insured) pay a premium capped by income, so the government pays every dollar of a change. Employer premiums (81.4% of the privately insured) are untaxed: when they fall, 85.0% comes back as taxable wages, taxed at 33.3% (income tax and both halves of payroll tax), and when they rise the reverse. Together: 31.6% of every premium dollar, so the new benefits and the copay cap cost the government $10.1B and the premium savings return $97.0B.
  • Workforce capacity investment (+$12.0B): Pays for 14,000 residency slots at $150,000 each, 10,000 nursing faculty, 60,000 clinical placements, 32,000 tuition-free service scholars, and 60,000 community health workers. Without it, the utilization increase turns into longer waits.

Reform 2: enhanced subsidies, reinsurance, and an individual mandate

National spending +$7.0B (+0.2%); federal +$24.5B; 28.3 million uninsured, against 32.2 million.

Close on coverage. It doesn't price this column's other rule changes (surprise billing, price transparency, PBMs, site-neutral payment), which mostly move prices rather than coverage.

Reform 5: a public option with automatic enrollment

National spending −$22.6B (−0.6%); federal +$122.1B; 6.6 million uninsured, against 32.2 million.

Close: a public option, automatic enrollment, and capped provider rates in the individual market. It doesn't cover undocumented immigrants, and its only drug-price change is larger rebates in the individual market.

Reform 6: Reform 5 with larger subsidies

National spending −$19.2B (−0.5%); federal +$161.8B; 6.6 million uninsured, against 32.2 million.

The nearest priced design, not a match: universal coverage through private and public plans, but employer plans, Medicaid, and Medicare stay. We found no published U.S. estimate of a Germany- or Switzerland-style system.

Reform 8: single payer with broad benefits and no cost sharing

National spending +$719.7B (+20.6%); federal +$2.84T; 0 million uninsured, against 32.2 million.

Close: everyone covered, no cost sharing, dental, vision, hearing, and long-term care. It pays hospitals 115% of Medicare rates and other providers Medicare rates. Its result differs sharply from the site's own model; the cost ledger shows the site's assumptions.
