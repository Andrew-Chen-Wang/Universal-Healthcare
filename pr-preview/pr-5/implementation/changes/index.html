Federal programs

WhatTodayUnder the planTrade-off
Medicare Part A (hospital insurance)
continues—
Covers inpatient, SNF, hospice, and some home health for 65+ and disabled; funded by the 2.9% HI payroll tax; deductible $1,676 per benefit period (2025).Continues as today, funded by the HI payroll tax.Seniors keep the program they know; its deductibles and gaps stay.
Medicare Part B (physician & outpatient)
continues—
Voluntary; $185/month premium (2025) plus income-related surcharges (IRMAA); 20% coinsurance with no cap.Continues; basic dental, glasses, and hearing aids added to match the standard benefit.Adds federal spending, up to $35.7B a year by CBO's estimate of a broader benefit.
Medicare Part C (Medicare Advantage)
amendedYear 1
About 54% of Medicare enrollees in private plans paid by capitation, with networks, prior authorization, and supplemental benefits.Continues with risk-score and audit fixes.Plans may trim extra benefits as overpayments shrink.
Medicare Part D (drug plans)
amendedYears 0–2
Standalone and MA drug plans run by private sponsors and PBMs; $2,000 out-of-pocket cap (2025); IRA negotiation of selected drugs.Continues; negotiated prices apply to more drugs.Lower drug costs; plan premiums may shift.
Medigap (federally standardized supplemental plans)
continues—
About 12.6M people pay roughly $2,000–4,000/yr to cover Medicare cost sharing.Continues.Supplemental cost stays for seniors who buy it.
Medicare Savings Programs & Part D Low-Income Subsidy
continues—
Means-tested help paying Medicare premiums and cost sharing; complex enrollment, low take-up.Continues for low-income seniors.No change in complexity.
Medicaid (acute care)
amendedYears 1–3
84.3M enrollees; state-run with federal match (FMAP); income eligibility varies by state (10 states have not expanded); ~75% in managed care; annual redeterminations.Continues, run by states, with continuous eligibility and at least the standard benefit; states may buy members into regulated plans.Keeps Medicaid's lower payments and access limits.
Medicaid long-term services (nursing homes, HCBS waivers)
continues—
The main U.S. payer for long-term care, but only after people spend down to poverty; HCBS waivers capped, with waiting lists.Continues as the main payer of long-term care until the long-term care decision.Families keep spending down to qualify.
Medicaid non-medical supports
continues—
Some states use Medicaid for housing supports and social-needs pilots (e.g., 1115 waivers).Continues.No change.
Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP)
amendedYear 1
7.3M children; separate state programs or Medicaid expansions; some premiums.Continues with continuous eligibility.Children's coverage stays separate from parents' plans in some states.
ACA Marketplaces (HealthCare.gov and state exchanges)
amendedYear 1
21.1M enrollees (2024) with premium tax credits; annual open enrollment; narrow networks.Become the regulated market: nonprofit plans only, one premium per region, with a public fallback plan.Some current plans leave; enrollees move without a gap.
ACA Basic Health Program (NY Essential Plan, MN MinnesotaCare, OR Bridge)
amendedYear 1
State programs for people just above Medicaid income.States may keep their Basic Health Programs as a regulated plan option.Variation between states continues.
Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) & Postal Service plan (PSHB)
continues—
About 8M federal employees, retirees, and families.Continues as the federal government's employer plan, meeting the standard benefit.No disruption for federal workers.
TRICARE (military dependents & retirees)
continues—
Defense Health Agency coverage for about 9.5M.Continues; coordination rules with regulated plans.Military families keep TRICARE.
Veterans Health Administration
continues—
Direct-care system for about 9M enrolled veterans; Community Care program pays outside providers.Continues; veterans may also enroll in a plan.Dual coverage adds coordination work.
Indian Health Service & tribal programs
expandedYear 4
Treaty-based care, chronically underfunded (per-capita spending far below other federal programs).Continues; tribal members may also enroll in a plan, which pays IHS and tribal providers.More funding for tribal providers; more billing.
Navigator and enrollment-assistance grants
expandedYears 0–3
Fund community groups to help people enroll in coverage.Expanded into in-person assisters for automatic enrollment.Costs money that reaches fewer people each year.
Medicare End-Stage Renal Disease program
continues—
Since 1972, Medicare covers dialysis and transplants for nearly everyone with kidney failure; private insurers pay first for 30 months and pay dialysis firms several times Medicare rates.Continues.No change.
Medicare hospice benefit
amendedYear 1
Per-diem payment for terminally ill patients who forgo curative treatment; rapid growth of for-profit hospices with documented fraud.Tighter oversight.Some for-profit hospices exit.
PACE (Programs of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly)
continues—
Capitated programs for frail elders eligible for nursing-home care, mostly dual eligibles.Continues.No change.
Dual Eligible Special Needs Plans (D-SNPs)
continues—
Medicare Advantage plans for people with both Medicare and Medicaid.Continue.No change.
Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program
continues—
Payer of last resort for HIV care, drugs (ADAP), and support services for low-income and uninsured people.Continues for services plans don't cover.No change.
Title X family planning program
continues—
Grants for family-planning services for low-income and uninsured people.Continues.No change.
SAMHSA mental health & substance use block grants
continues—
Fund services for uninsured people and services insurers do not cover.Continue.No change.
ACA cost-sharing reductions & silver loading
amendedYear 1
Lower deductibles for low-income Marketplace enrollees; insurers load the cost into silver premiums.Replaced by the income-based cost-sharing cap in every regulated plan.Ends silver loading.
Vaccines for Children & adult vaccine coverage
continues—
VFC buys vaccines for Medicaid and uninsured children; adult coverage varies by payer.Continue; adult vaccines in the standard benefit.No change.
Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS)
continues—
Runs Medicare, oversees state Medicaid and CHIP programs and the federal Marketplace.Continues running Medicare and Medicaid; the new regulator oversees the regulated plans.Two federal agencies to coordinate.
AHRQ & PCORI (comparative-effectiveness research)
amendedYear 0
Fund comparative-effectiveness research; PCORI is barred from using cost-per-QALY thresholds for coverage.Feed evidence to the technology-assessment institute.No change in funding.
National Institutes of Health
continues—
The largest public funder of biomedical research.Continues.No change.
CDC and state & local public health departments
continues—
Disease surveillance, vaccination programs, outbreak response.Continues.No change.
DOL Employee Benefits Security Administration (health plan oversight)
amendedYear 0
Oversees about 2.5 million employer health plans under ERISA.Enforces the standard benefit and denial rules in employer plans.More oversight of employer plans.
Office of Health Equity (new)
newYear 0
Disparities monitoring is scattered across agencies.An equity office in the regulator tracks gaps by plan type.Small cost.
Office of Primary Care (new)
newYear 0
No federal entity is responsible for primary-care capacity.A primary-care office in the regulator.Small cost.
24-month Medicare waiting period for people on disability (SSDI)
continues—
People approved for SSDI wait two years for Medicare.Continues, but people waiting are enrolled in regulated plans.Covered during the wait, through a plan.
Formal consultation with patients, providers, states, and tribes
newYear 0
Rulemaking comment periods and advisory committees.Formal consultation with patients, providers, states, and tribes.Slower rulemaking.

Federal tax code

WhatTodayUnder the planTrade-off
IRC §106 exclusion of employer health coverage
continues—
Employer premiums are excluded from income and payroll tax: the largest federal tax expenditure (~$400B/yr incl. payroll).The tax exclusion for employer coverage continues.Keeps a large tax break weighted toward higher earners.
IRC §125 cafeteria plans & Flexible Spending Accounts
continues—
Pre-tax premium payments and FSAs for out-of-pocket costs.Continues.No change.
Health Savings Accounts (IRC §223) and high-deductible plans
amendedYear 1
About 38M accounts; triple tax advantage; requires a high-deductible plan.Continue, paired with plans that meet the income-based cost-sharing cap.Tax-favored savings remain most useful to higher earners.
HRAs, ICHRA & QSEHRA
continues—
Employer accounts to reimburse individual premiums and costs.Continue; ICHRA lets employers fund regulated-plan premiums.Some employers shift workers to the regulated market.
IRC §36B premium tax credits
expandedYear 1
$116.6B in 2024 for Marketplace coverage.Becomes the income-based premium subsidy for everyone in the regulated plans.Federal cost rises; paid by top-end taxes.
Employer mandate (IRC §4980H)
continues—
Large employers must offer coverage or pay a penalty.Continues for employers with 50+ full-time workers, with the standard benefit as the coverage test.Employers near the threshold keep facing a cost cliff.
Medical expense deduction (IRC §213) and self-employed health insurance deduction (§162(l))
continues—
Itemized deduction above 7.5% of AGI; self-employed deduct premiums.Continues.No change.
Top-end surtax, capital-gains change, financial transactions tax (new)
newYear 1
Top rate 37%; long-term capital gains 20% (+3.8% NIIT).A surtax on income above $1,000,000 and capital gains taxed as ordinary income above $1,000,000; no financial transactions tax.Only very high incomes pay; revenue is volatile.
Nonprofit hospital tax exemption & charity care rules (IRC §501(r))
continues—
Nonprofit hospitals are exempt from income and property taxes in exchange for 'community benefit', much of it counted as charity care and Medicaid shortfall.Continues; charity-care rules matter less as the uninsured are covered.Hospitals' charity-care obligations shrink in practice.
IRS health-coverage reporting (Forms 1095-A/B/C)
amendedYear 1
Insurers and employers report coverage for ACA subsidies and the employer mandate.Coverage reporting feeds automatic enrollment and subsidies.Same forms, new uses.

Federal insurance & health law

WhatTodayUnder the planTrade-off
COBRA continuation coverage
amendedYear 1
Lets people keep employer coverage after job loss at up to 102% of premium.Replaced in practice by portable coverage at the regulated premium.Ex-employers stop administering COBRA; people pay a regulated premium.
ERISA (employer benefit plans) & its preemption of state law (§514)
amendedYear 0
Self-insured employer plans (about 60% of covered workers) are federally regulated and shielded from state insurance law.Amended so self-insured employer plans meet the standard benefit, cost-sharing cap, and denial rules.Large employers lose some plan-design freedom.
ACA insurance market rules (guaranteed issue, community rating, essential health benefits, MLR)
expandedYear 0
Govern the individual and small-group markets.Extended: nonprofit status, administrative-cost limits, and one premium per region for the standard benefit.Insurers lose profit on the standard benefit.
EMTALA (emergency treatment requirement)
continues—
Hospitals must screen and stabilize anyone, which generates uncompensated care.Continues; uninsured patients are enrolled at the point of care.Hospitals get paid for emergency care they gave free before.
No Surprises Act & independent dispute resolution
amendedYear 0
Limits surprise out-of-network bills; arbitration between insurers and providers.Arbitration replaced by a default out-of-network rate.Specialists lose leverage in disputes.
HIPAA (privacy, security, transaction standards)
amendedYear 0
Governs covered entities and standard electronic transactions across thousands of payers.Extended to cover the eligibility service, risk adjustment, and health apps.More compliance work.
Medicare Secondary Payer rules & coordination of benefits
amendedYear 4
Complex rules decide which insurer pays first.Coordination rules updated for regulated plans.Coordination remains complex.
Hyde Amendment
continues—
Annual appropriations rider barring federal funds for most abortions.Continues as under current law for federal funds.Unchanged political compromise.
ACA and Medicare Advantage risk adjustment
expandedYear 0
Transfers between insurers based on enrollee health; drives coding intensity.A national risk-adjustment fund across all regulated plans.Invites upcoding; needs audits.
Short-term limited-duration plans, fixed indemnity plans & health care sharing ministries
amendedYear 1
Cheaper, underwritten coverage outside ACA rules; sharing ministries are not insurance.Can't be sold as a substitute for the standard benefit.Cheap plans for healthy people disappear.
Coverage of routine costs in clinical trials
continues—
Required for Medicare and ACA plans, but patients still face network and prior-authorization barriers.Routine costs covered in the standard benefit.No change.
Non-discrimination by participating providers
expandedYear 1
ACA Section 1557 and civil rights law apply unevenly; providers can refuse patients by insurance type.Plans and providers may not discriminate by plan type in emergencies and urgent care.Limits how much faster employer-plan patients can be seen for urgent care.
National plan regulator (new)
newYear 0
Plan rules are split among 50 state insurance departments, ERISA for self-insured plans, and CMS for the Marketplaces.One national regulator sets the standard benefit, approves plans, runs risk adjustment, and enforces administrative-cost limits.One rulebook, but a single target for lobbying.

Provider payment & regulation

WhatTodayUnder the planTrade-off
Medicare hospital payment systems (IPPS DRGs, OPPS)
continues—
Per-case and per-service rates for Medicare; commercial insurers pay negotiated prices about 2.5x Medicare.Medicare's payment systems continue and become the reference for the commercial cap.Medicare rates now matter for every payer's ceiling.
Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (RBRVS) & the AMA RUC
continues—
Medicare fees anchor all payers; relative values largely developed by the AMA's RUC committee.Continues; the reference for the commercial cap.Fee-schedule politics affect more money.
Medicaid & Medicare DSH and supplemental payments
amendedYears 1–4
Billions in disproportionate-share and supplemental payments compensate hospitals for uninsured and Medicaid patients.Reduced as uncompensated care falls.Safety-net hospitals lose payments they relied on.
340B Drug Pricing Program
amendedYears 0–2
Safety-net hospitals and clinics buy outpatient drugs at deep discounts and keep the spread from insurer reimbursement (tens of billions a year).Continues; negotiated prices narrow the spread hospitals earn.Hospitals lose some 340B revenue.
Medicare Graduate Medical Education funding
expandedYear 0
Medicare funds most residency positions, capped since 1997 apart from recent small additions.More funded residency slots.Paid years before new doctors practice.
State certificate-of-need laws
continues—
About 35 states require approval for some facility investments.Continues; capacity plans guide where it applies.Can slow new entrants.
Stark law & Anti-Kickback Statute
amendedYear 0
Fraud and abuse laws for Medicare and Medicaid.Extended to related-party payments that could evade administrative limits.More compliance work for integrated systems.
Hospital and insurer price transparency rules
amendedYear 0
Hospitals and insurers must publish negotiated rates.Enforced, and prices published against the commercial cap.Hospitals publish more data.
Prior authorization & utilization management
amendedYear 0
Payer-specific rules; major administrative burden.Limited to a short list, with gold-carding and one national standard.Some low-value care gets through.
Medicare ACOs & CMS Innovation Center models
continues—
Value-based payment experiments layered on fee-for-service.Continue.No change.
Medical malpractice (state tort law)
continues—
Litigation-based compensation; premiums are a cost of practice.Continues; optional no-fault pilots.Pilots may be too small to change much.
Critical Access Hospitals (cost-based reimbursement)
continues—
About 1,370 small rural hospitals are paid 101% of reasonable cost by Medicare instead of prospective rates.Cost-based reimbursement continues; exceptions to the cap.No change for rural hospitals.
FQHC and Rural Health Clinic prospective payment (encounter rates)
continues—
Health centers get a per-visit rate from Medicare and Medicaid, plus Section 330 grants for uninsured care.Continues; more sites funded.No change.
Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics
continues—
Clinics paid a cost-based daily or monthly rate under Medicaid demonstrations.Continue and expand with capacity grants.No change.
Antitrust enforcement for hospital and physician mergers
amendedYear 0
FTC challenges some mergers because consolidation raises commercial prices.Stronger merger enforcement.Hospitals lose some bargaining power.
Site-of-service payment differences (hospital outpatient vs. physician office)
amendedYear 0
The same service pays more when billed by a hospital outpatient department, which encourages hospitals to buy practices.Site-neutral payment for services safely done outside hospitals.Hospitals lose facility-fee revenue.
ACA ban on new physician-owned hospitals
continues—
Section 6001 bars new physician-owned hospitals from Medicare.Continues.No change.
Telehealth coverage rules
amendedYear 0
Medicare telehealth flexibilities repeatedly extended on short-term deadlines; private coverage varies.Covered at parity in the standard benefit.Risk of added visits rather than replaced ones.
Organ transplant financing (organ acquisition cost centers)
continues—
Medicare reimburses transplant hospitals for organ acquisition costs through cost reports; patients need insurance to be listed.Continues.No change.
Hospital uncompensated care and charity care policies
amendedYears 1–4
Hospitals provide tens of billions of dollars a year in charity care and absorb bad debt.Falls as the uninsured are covered.Hospitals rely less on charity funds.
Medicare hospital cost reports
continues—
Annual cost reports, used mainly for Medicare policy, are often unreliable.Continue; inform cap exceptions.No change.
Hospital and practice billing departments
amendedYears 0–2
Departments for coding, claims, denials, prior authorization, and patient collections across many payers.Simpler with one claim format and eligibility service.Billing jobs fall somewhat.
Distressed-hospital support (state bailouts, HHS Provider Relief-style funds)
amendedYears 1–4
Ad hoc rescue funds for distressed hospitals.Cap exceptions for rural and safety-net hospitals instead of a transition fund.Exceptions reduce savings.
Licensed mental health counselors and marriage & family therapists
amendedYear 1
Medicare began paying them only in 2024; Medicaid and private coverage vary.Counted in network adequacy for mental health.Plans must contract more counselors.
Medicare pay-for-performance programs (MIPS, Hospital Value-Based Purchasing, readmission penalties)
continues—
Small bonuses and penalties with heavy reporting burden and mixed evidence of benefit.Continue in Medicare.No change.
Cap on commercial prices (new)
newYears 1–4
Commercial prices are negotiated plan by plan; hospitals charge employer plans far above Medicare.Commercial and employer plans may not pay more than 200% of Medicare, phased in.Lower employer premiums vs. revenue for high-priced hospitals.

Drugs & devices

WhatTodayUnder the planTrade-off
Pharmacy benefit managers
amendedYear 0
Three PBMs process about 80% of prescriptions; rebates, spread pricing, formulary placement.Regulated: pay delinked from drug prices, no spread pricing.PBM profits fall.
Medicare Drug Price Negotiation (IRA)
expandedYears 0–2
Up to 15–20 drugs a year negotiated for Medicare only.Expanded to more drugs, with prices applying to every payer.Somewhat fewer new drugs over decades.
Physician-administered drugs (Medicare Part B ASP+6%)
amendedYears 0–2
Paid at average sales price + 6%, which rewards using pricier drugs.Negotiated prices replace average sales price for negotiated drugs.Lower margins for practices that administer drugs.
Manufacturer rebates and Medicaid Drug Rebate Program
amendedYears 0–2
Opaque rebate system; Medicaid gets statutory rebates and 'best price'.Rebates shrink as net prices are negotiated directly; Medicaid rebates continue.Less hidden pricing.
Medical devices & durable medical equipment pricing
continues—
Hospitals and payers negotiate device prices; Medicare competitive bidding for some DME.Continues; technology assessment informs coverage.Little change.
Group purchasing organizations & drug wholesalers
continues—
GPOs negotiate hospital supply prices and collect administrative fees from suppliers; three wholesalers distribute most drugs.Continue.No change.
Biosimilar and generic substitution
amendedYears 0–2
Slow biosimilar uptake because rebates favor brand biologics.Encouraged through negotiation and substitution rules.Brand makers lose share.
FDA drug and device approval
continues—
Approval is based on safety and efficacy; price and comparative value are not considered.Continues.No change.
