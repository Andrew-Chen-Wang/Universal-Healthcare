Net cost of health insurance

Premiums minus benefits paid: insurer administration, marketing, broker commissions, premium taxes, and profit. For Medicare and Medicaid this is mostly Medicare Advantage, Part D, and Medicaid managed-care plan overhead.

Why this cost exists

Private insurance grew from the Baylor hospital plan of 1929, which became Blue Cross, and the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 left its regulation to the states, so there are hundreds of insurers with their own rules. Each charges for administration, marketing, broker commissions, taxes, and profit on top of the care it pays for. The ACA's medical loss ratio rule (2011) caps that overhead as a share of premiums. Public programs add their own private-plan overhead: Medicare Advantage (rooted in the 1982 TEFRA risk contracts and the 2003 MMA) and Medicaid managed care, which the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 let states require.

Net cost is premiums minus claims, so it swings with how claims compare with what insurers expected. It rose 25.5% in 2020, when people put off care while premiums were already set, and insurers' profits spiked. It fell 2.4% in 2024 as Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans paid far more in claims than they had planned for, and as the Medicaid "unwinding" shrank managed-care enrollment. Over the long run it grows with premiums and with the shift of Medicare and Medicaid enrollees into private plans.

What made it jump, drop, or change speed

  1. 1
    1966–1968 Medicare and Medicaid begin

    Medicaid started in 1966 and Medicare in July 1966, paying hospitals their costs and paying doctors their customary fees. Hospital and nursing home spending sped up at once, and Medicaid began paying for dental care in many states; spending through private insurance slowed because Medicare took over the over-65s' bills.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): +100.0% a year, 1966–1968
    CMS Office of the Actuary (2025)
  2. 2
    1974–1977 Controls end: prices catch up

    When controls ended in April 1974, hospital and physician prices rose quickly to catch up, and insurers raised premiums sharply in 1976–1977 after paying more in claims than they had priced for.

    • Net cost of private health insurance: +20.6% a year, 1973–1977, vs +14.2% a year before
    • Net cost of health insurance: +19.1% a year, 1973–1977, vs +12.9% a year before
    Health Care Financing Administration (1994)
  3. 3
    1982–1984 Medicaid managed care begins

    OBRA 1981 let states require Medicaid enrollees to join managed-care plans, and in October 1982 Arizona launched AHCCCS, the first statewide Medicaid managed-care program, which made plan overhead a new line of Medicaid spending.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): +50.5% a year, 1981–1984, vs +5.3% a year before
    Arizona Health Care Cost Containment System (2020)
  4. 4
    1982–1987 Medicare HMO contracts

    After early demonstration projects, TEFRA (1982) created Medicare risk contracts, in force from 1985, that pay an HMO a fixed monthly amount per enrollee; plan overhead grew from almost nothing to a steady line by the late 1980s.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +110.5% a year, 1981–1987
  5. 5
    1988–1992 Insurers' underwriting losses, then catch-up premiums

    Health insurance runs in a cycle of roughly six years: insurers underprice to win business, take losses, then raise premiums faster than claims to restore margins. Losses in the mid-1980s were followed by fast premium increases and rising overhead from 1988.

    • Net cost of private health insurance: +17.9% a year, 1987–1992, vs +4.1% a year before
    • Net cost of health insurance: +16.6% a year, 1987–1992, vs +4.0% a year before
  6. 6
    1989–1994 States move Medicaid into managed care

    Using 1915(b) and 1115 waivers, states moved Medicaid enrollees into managed-care plans through the 1990s, from under a tenth of enrollees in 1991 to about half by 1998, so plan overhead grew far faster than Medicaid itself.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): +39.2% a year, 1988–1994, vs +20.0% a year before
  7. 7
    1994–1998 Insurers' margins squeezed by managed-care price wars

    In the mid-1990s insurers competed hard on price to win employers moving to managed care, holding premiums close to claims, so the net cost of private insurance stopped growing.

    • Net cost of private health insurance: +0.9% a year, 1993–1998, vs +15.5% a year before
    • Net cost of health insurance: +3.2% a year, 1993–1998, vs +15.6% a year before
  8. 8
    1997–2000 Balanced Budget Act: home health collapse and Medicare+Choice cuts

    The 1997 Balanced Budget Act's interim payment system for home health (from October 1997) halved Medicare home health spending from 1996 to 1999 and closed many agencies. The same law slowed payments to Medicare HMOs, and many plans left the program.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +11.7% a year, 1996–2000, vs +29.2% a year before
    HHS ASPE (2003)
  9. 9
    2001–2004 Managed-care backlash: premiums surge

    After a backlash against HMO restrictions, employers moved to looser networks and state laws limited managed-care tactics; premium growth returned to double digits from 2001 to about 2004, and insurers' margins peaked in 2002 as premiums outpaced claims.

    • Net cost of private health insurance: +15.2% a year, 2000–2004, vs +6.0% a year before
    • Net cost of health insurance: +15.7% a year, 2000–2004, vs +6.8% a year before
    Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2005); CMS Office of the Actuary (2004)
  10. 10
    2004–2009 Medicare Modernization Act raises private-plan payments

    The 2003 law raised payments to Medicare private plans (renamed Medicare Advantage) by about a tenth in 2004, and enrollment and plan overhead grew quickly until 2009.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +35.5% a year, 2003–2009, vs −4.7% a year before
    Commonwealth Fund (2004)
  11. 11
    2006 Medicare Part D begins

    Medicare's drug benefit started in January 2006. Medicare's private-plan overhead roughly doubled, enrollees began paying Part D premiums, general revenue for Medicare jumped, employers began receiving the retiree drug subsidy, and states began paying Medicare back for dual eligibles' drugs (the 'clawback,' counted in state programs) while their own Medicaid drug spending fell.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +110.6% in 2006, vs +12.8% a year before
    CMS Office of the Actuary (2025)
  12. 12
    2010–2015 ACA cuts Medicare Advantage benchmarks

    The ACA phased down payments to Medicare Advantage plans from 2011, closer to the cost of traditional Medicare, and plan overhead grew slowly until about 2015 even as enrollment kept rising.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +6.7% a year, 2009–2015, vs +35.4% a year before
    JAMA Health Forum (2023)
  13. 13
    2014–2015 ACA coverage expansion

    The marketplaces opened with premium tax credits, Medicaid expanded to low-income adults in participating states (mostly through managed-care plans), and insurers began paying the ACA's health insurer fee.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): +37.2% a year, 2013–2015, vs +15.9% a year before
    • Net cost of private health insurance: +4.8% a year, 2013–2015, vs +2.1% a year before
  14. 14
    2015–2019 Medicare Advantage grows again

    Medicare Advantage enrollment grew every year as plans added extra benefits, funded partly by quality bonuses and by coding more diagnoses, and plan overhead grew faster from 2015.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicare (mostly Medicare Advantage and Part D plans): +9.1% a year, 2014–2019, vs +5.6% a year before
  15. 15
    2020 COVID-19 pandemic

    Federal emergency money poured in: the Provider Relief Fund and Paycheck Protection Program loans to providers (counted in other federal programs), testing and vaccines (public health), and a higher federal Medicaid match in exchange for keeping everyone enrolled. Meanwhile people put off care, so payments for dental, equipment, and out-of-pocket care fell, insurers' claims dropped while premiums held, and health care lost jobs.

    • Net cost of health insurance: +25.5% in 2020, vs +4.9% a year before
    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): +50.9% in 2020, vs +3.9% a year before
    CMS Office of the Actuary (2021)
  16. 16
    2021 Delayed care returns

    Patients came back for dental work, equipment, therapy, and mental health care put off in 2020, so those lines grew far faster than usual; nursing homes, which lost residents and staff, shrank; insurers' net cost fell as claims rebounded; and building projects paused in 2020 resumed.

    • Net cost of private health insurance: −12.4% in 2021, vs +8.3% a year before
  17. 17
    2023–2024 Medicaid unwinding

    The pandemic coverage guarantee ended in 2023 and states rechecked every enrollee; millions lost coverage, the extra federal match phased out over 2023, so the state share jumped in 2024 while the federal share barely grew, managed-care overhead fell, and state administrative costs rose.

    • Net cost of insurance, Medicaid (mostly managed-care plans): −11.0% a year, 2022–2024, vs +11.0% a year before
Sources: CMS Office of the Actuary (2025)
